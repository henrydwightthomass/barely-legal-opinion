Louisville's History of Racial Discrimination

How generations of discrimination shaped the city we know today.

By Henry Thomas October 2026 Issue 002 Politics
002

Old enough to vote.
Young enough to live with the consequences.

Louisville, my hometown. A city with a rich history, famous landmarks, vibrant local life, unique industries, and the two most exciting minutes in sports.

I love my hometown dearly. It has much to be proud of, much to explore, and countless perspectives to encounter. But for as long as I can remember, one question has lingered in my mind: Why are certain parts of the city the way they are?

Of course, socioeconomic differences exist everywhere. Drive through nearly any major American city, and you'll notice disparities between neighborhoods. But even as a child, I found myself questioning these differences on a deeper level. Why did some neighborhoods seem to flourish while others struggled? Why did opportunity appear so abundant in certain parts of the city and so distant in others? And perhaps most importantly, how did things get this way?

Now, let me be clear: there is no single explanation for these disparities. Cities are complicated, shaped by generations of economic, political, and social decisions. But as I've grown older and begun examining the history of the place I call home, I've discovered something that has fundamentally changed how I understand Louisville.

Racism.

The word itself was hardly unfamiliar to me. I've spent considerable time studying America's racial history, from slavery and the writings of Frederick Douglass to Jim Crow and the civil rights activists who helped shape the world we know today. Yet, for much of my life, I understood these injustices primarily through the lens of our nation's history, without fully recognizing how deeply they were woven into the history of the streets, neighborhoods, and communities surrounding me.

I knew America's history. But how well did I really know my own city, or how its history connects to our nation’s?

There are countless ways I could approach this topic, but it only seems right to begin with one of the communities most deeply shaped by Louisville's racial history: the West End.

Today, the West End is home to some of Louisville's most economically disadvantaged neighborhoods. But to understand the challenges these communities face today, we must first understand the history that preceded them.

During the early and mid-20th century, Louisville was home to a thriving network of Black entrepreneurship. Along downtown's historic Walnut Street, just east of the West End, Black-owned businesses helped create a flourishing center of commerce, culture, and opportunity. In 1932, city directories listed 154 Black-owned businesses in the district, ranging from banks and insurance companies to theaters, restaurants, and medical offices.

But while these communities were building wealth and pursuing successful futures, the city was passing racial laws that restricted where Black residents could live. In 1914, Louisville passed a residential segregation ordinance that prevented Black people from moving into majority-white neighborhoods and white people from doing the same in majority-Black neighborhoods. The city said it was to preserve “public peace.” The U.S. Supreme Court struck down the ordinance in the landmark 1917 case *Buchanan v. Warley*, ruling that it violated property rights. But striking down the ordinance did not undo the damage it had caused. And as you will read, this was far from the end of Louisville’s history of discriminatory policies and practices.

You can’t take on this topic without discussing something that affected Black communities in Louisville beginning in the 1930s and arguably still has consequences across the nation today: redlining.

In 1937, the Home Owners' Loan Corporation classified Louisville neighborhoods with grades from A to D, with green A-rated neighborhoods considered the most desirable and red D-rated neighborhoods considered hazardous. (Depicted above) Race was a factor in these assessments, and a large portion of what we call West Louisville today received the lowest classification. This is extremely important to understanding what we see today. The neighborhood of Russell was the only majority-Black neighborhood to receive a grade above D, and even then, it was classified as “definitely declining.” On the other hand, Chickasaw, a highly desirable and predominantly white neighborhood at the time, reportedly lost its “best” rating because of its proximity to a “Negro park.” When diving into these documents and explanations, the rhetoric used is one of blatant racism and discrimination. This had a ripple effect, limiting the options available to many Black residents of these neighborhoods. Look at it like this: say there were two families from similar financial backgrounds. One lived in an area deemed “most desirable” and wanted to apply for a mortgage. That family would be more likely to qualify for an affordable mortgage, purchase a home, pay off the loan over time, and build equity. They could borrow against that equity or sell the home, potentially leaving more wealth for the next generation. The family with a similar financial background living in a neighborhood redlined as hazardous might struggle to qualify for a loan at all. Even if they did qualify, unfavorable terms could make the mortgage harder to pay off, limiting their ability to accumulate equity and pass housing wealth down. And that is assuming they could get a loan. If they could not, they might have to rent instead, losing an important opportunity to build generational wealth through homeownership. This also restricts the ability to invest in improvements and increase property value in existing homes because of the lack of affordable financing. This can create a cycle of deteriorating housing, lower property values, and declining investment in communities. A 2021 study in the American Economic Journal: Economic Policy found that historical HOLC map classifications contributed to lower homeownership and property values in affected neighborhoods. This also affected the Black entrepreneurship discussed earlier. A homeowner may be able to use accumulated equity as collateral for a business loan. Someone denied the opportunity to own a home may not have that option. When household wealth is restricted and investors are pushed away from certain neighborhoods, entrepreneurship becomes more difficult.

As West Louisville became increasingly Black, white flight occurred on a large scale. By the end of the 1960s, approximately 15,000 white residents had left Louisville’s West End, according to historical accounts. This happened for several reasons, such as suburbanization, racial prejudice, and a method called blockbusting.

Blockbusting was a method used by realtors to pressure white families into selling their homes by exploiting fears that Black families moving nearby would cause property values to decline. Many white families reportedly sold their homes and taking financial losses because they felt pressured to sell quickly. Realtors and speculators could then purchase these homes, resell them for a profit, and repeat the cycle.

White residents also tried to prevent more Black people from moving to the West End, particularly in Shawnee. In 1950, approximately 400 white families started the Shawnee Homeowners Association to oppose further Black migration into the neighborhood. As these efforts failed to prevent demographic changes, white outmigration became increasingly prevalent in the late 1950s and 1960s.

Now, you may be asking why so many white people were able to move out and relocate to other neighborhoods while Black families faced far greater restrictions on where they could live. The answer lies partly in racial discrimination, which prevented many Black Americans from accessing the same services and financial opportunities as white Americans.

Following World War II, the federal government introduced another policy that would help reshape the American housing market: the GI Bill of 1944. Through its home loan guaranty program, millions of returning veterans gained access to mortgages with favorable terms, often requiring little or no down payment. By 1955, approximately 4.3 million home loans had been granted nationwide under the program.

Yet the opportunities created by this legislation were not equally accessible. Although Black veterans were legally eligible, discriminatory lending practices and racial restrictions in suburban housing markets frequently prevented them from obtaining the same benefits as white veterans.

Louisville was no exception to the broader pattern of suburban migration. In its 2013 Shawnee neighborhood plan, Louisville Metro specifically identified the GI Bill as one factor helping residents relocate to suburban communities after World War II.

The result was a striking contrast: while federal mortgage guarantees helped many veterans purchase homes in expanding suburbs, discriminatory housing practices continued limiting where many Black veterans could purchase property and accumulate wealth.

The GI Bill wasn’t the only factor. The FHA also played a major role. In 1934, during the Great Depression, President Franklin D. Roosevelt's administration established the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) through the National Housing Act.

Before the modern mortgage system developed, many home loans required substantial down payments, had relatively short repayment periods, and sometimes required borrowers to make large balloon payments. FHA insurance helped make private mortgage loans more accessible.

The process would work something like this. A family would apply to a bank for a mortgage. The bank would evaluate the borrower and the property, and if the mortgage qualified for FHA insurance, the federal government would protect the lender against certain losses. With less financial risk, mortgage lenders could offer longer repayment periods and smaller down payments.

This sounds like a good idea, and for many Americans, it was a good resource. But the FHA, in its 1938 Underwriting Manual, used racially discriminatory rhetoric throughout.

The manual stated, “If a neighborhood is to retain stability, it is necessary that properties shall continue to be occupied by the same social and racial classes.”

It also warned about the “infiltration of inharmonious racial groups,” as well as treating the racial composition of schools as a factor in neighborhood desirability, referring to what it called “inharmonious racial groups.”

This discriminatory language helps explain the unequal distribution of FHA-backed mortgages. According to a historical national estimate cited in congressional testimony, approximately 98% of the dollar value of FHA loans distributed between 1934 and 1962 went to white families, with about 2% going to nonwhite families. While this statistic alone does not prove discrimination in every lending decision, the FHA's own underwriting policies provide direct evidence of the racial prejudice embedded within the system.

For Black Americans, this meant that the color of their skin could influence how financial institutions assessed the neighborhoods in which they lived, regardless of their individual qualifications or financial responsibility.

The government was helping make homeownership more accessible to millions of Americans while simultaneously endorsing practices that restricted access to those opportunities for others.

This is another example of how unequal access to government-supported resources restricted many Black families’ ability to relocate, purchase property, and accumulate wealth.

But there was another problem. Even if these Black business owners or residents in the West End had the financial means to relocate, where exactly were they supposed to go? The simple answer would be to move elsewhere in the city, but the reality wasn’t so simple. Meet the Wade family. Andrew Wade IV, a Black veteran and electrician, and his wife, Charlotte Wade, wanted to move into a nice suburban area and had the financial means to do so. Buying a home was already difficult enough. They faced extreme racial discrimination in the housing market and could not purchase the house directly. Two white civil rights activists, Anne and Carl Braden, bought the home with the funds transferred to them by the Wade family. The home was located at 4010 Rone Court (now Clyde Drive) in Shively, Kentucky. The family moved in, and immediately, the racism of this predominantly white area became apparent. The local dairy provider refused to provide them with milk because of their skin color, and their newspaper delivery was canceled because the carrier refused to serve them. Their original home insurance was also canceled. Those were some of the ways local businesses discriminated against them, but the hostility from people in the surrounding community was even worse. A cross was burned near their home, rocks were thrown through the windows, gunshots were fired into their house, and they received many threats intended to get them out of the neighborhood. June 27, 1954, marked one of the clearest and most heinous racist acts in Louisville’s history. At approximately 12:30 a.m., dynamite exploded under the room of the Wades’ 2-year-old daughter, Rosemary. Fortunately, no one was injured because the family was away at the time. The explosion destroyed the rear portion of the house. The bombing ultimately forced the family from the home, although Andrew initially refused to give up the property. The family eventually returned to the West End. What happened to the person responsible for bombing their house? No one was ever convicted. Instead, police focused their investigation on the activists who had helped the Wades purchase the home. To add to this, the victim, Andrew Wade, was arrested for protecting his house about a month later. Yes, protecting his house. Andrew initially refused to give up the home and returned to protect it as racial threats continued. He brought friends with him to the house at night to accompany him. Police demanded a list of names of the friends he would bring to the property. Andrew refused and was arrested on charges of breach of the peace.

Redlining, unequal access to resources, and white flight were not the only forces shaping Louisville's Black community. While white families were moving into the suburbs, a transformation was taking place in the city itself, one that would permanently alter the city, its neighborhoods, and the businesses and institutions that had been built by Black Louisvillians for generations. This was called “urban renewal,” and it was presented as a promising idea. For people who weren’t directly affected, the concept probably sounded appealing: demolish deteriorating buildings and replace them with new development. The problem was that many of the homes and businesses being destroyed in Louisville were Black-owned or occupied by Black tenants.

In the 1960s, the Walnut Street district, which at its peak featured over 150 Black businesses, was largely flattened, as reported by WDRB. The University of Louisville Libraries confirms that urban renewal wiped out most of the businesses and residential properties in the district during the 1960s. A historic preservation report prepared for the Kentucky Heritage Council describes how the area was targeted for clearance and replacement with government buildings and private developments. The report specifically identifies the destruction of African American landmarks, housing, and the Walnut Street business district, followed by the relocation of Black residents farther west. University of Louisville researchers found that Black homes and neighborhoods were disproportionately designated for clearance. They also note that photographs of homes slated for demolition sometimes showed little obvious distinction from homes selected for preservation.

What happened to the Black business owners who had spent years, sometimes decades, building their livelihoods in these neighborhoods? Were they able to simply open their businesses somewhere else? Historical research by Luther Adams found that few Black business owners could afford to relocate, and those who could faced the additional obstacle of finding property in a segregated housing market. A Louisville Metro historical report similarly acknowledges that urban renewal planners failed to account for the fact that many Black business owners rented their commercial spaces and could not afford to relocate. WDRB reported that many businesses that did manage to relocate ultimately failed. One example was Frank Moorman, a Black entrepreneur who helped establish a service station at Eighth and Walnut in 1937. His business was located in an area later targeted by the West Walnut Street Urban Renewal Project, and his surviving archival records document the project. While the exact number of Walnut Street businesses that permanently closed remains unclear, the consequences of their destruction extended far beyond the buildings themselves. These businesses represented generations of Black entrepreneurship, employment, and economic opportunity. Even when property owners received compensation, how could that alone replace the customers, relationships, and community institutions that had taken decades to establish?

And the consequences of urban renewal did not end with the destruction of these buildings. According to the University of Louisville Libraries, these projects contributed to pushing Black communities farther west, away from downtown and the predominantly white neighborhoods of Louisville's East End. Louisville Metro's historical research also documents the difficulties displaced residents faced in finding replacement housing. This meant that while white families were increasingly moving into suburban neighborhoods, many Black families were being displaced from their existing communities without the same opportunities to relocate elsewhere. West Louisville already had established Black communities, but urban renewal contributed to concentrating more displaced residents in these neighborhoods. What makes this particularly troubling is that the same communities already facing the consequences of redlining and unequal investment were now being expected to absorb more residents who had lost their homes and businesses.

Urban renewal, white flight, discriminatory acts, and legislation were not the only things disproportionately affecting the Black community of Louisville; another form of development was creating further division and displacement within these communities. The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 expanded interstate highway construction nationwide, and Louisville was no exception. Research shows that the construction of I-64 and the Ninth Street ramps contributed to disruption, displacement, and division, with impacts that can still be seen today.

But how exactly did the construction of these highways affect the communities surrounding them? According to Louisville Metro's 20-Year Action Plan, the construction of the interstate freeway created a divide at Ninth Street, separating downtown from the historically Black Russell neighborhood. The same report acknowledges that urban renewal and highway development contributed to the destruction of the once-thriving African American business district along Walnut Street, which extended from Sixth to Thirteenth Street. But this division was not simply a matter of geography. A report by the American Institute of Architects compared aerial photographs of Ninth Street from 1952 and 2014, revealing how what was once an ordinary neighborhood street had been transformed into a multi-lane highway corridor connecting to Interstate 64. Today, Ninth Street consists of six lanes, with three running in each direction, creating a physical barrier between downtown and West Louisville. In fact, Louisville Metro's own 2022 federal grant application acknowledges that interstate construction and urban renewal displaced Black-owned businesses, cultural institutions, and residences, contributing to economic decline in the surrounding communities. What is particularly striking is that the city is now investing nearly $25 million into a project called Reimagine Ninth Street, including $15.6 million in federal grant funding awarded in 2022, to reduce this divide and reconnect these neighborhoods. The city plans to transform Ninth Street from a six-lane thoroughfare into a narrower, more accessible roadway. Decades after these communities were divided in the name of development, millions of dollars are now being invested to reconnect them. But while a roadway can be redesigned, how does a city replace the businesses, homes, and economic opportunities that were lost along the way?

The effects of these developments went beyond destruction and displacement. They also intersected with something much more personal: residents’ health. As construction and industrialization reshaped Louisville’s landscape, pollution created another problem that continues to affect surrounding communities. This brings us to a form of inequality that often gets overlooked: environmental racism, whose effects have been studied in western Louisville.

To understand this, we have to understand the industrial area of Rubbertown. Rubbertown developed into a major center for synthetic rubber production in western Louisville during World War II, when the United States needed alternatives to natural rubber for military and industrial purposes. Now, let me be clear: these developments provided countless jobs for the city of Louisville and contributed to Louisville's economic growth. But this industrial presence would remain for generations. As these industries contributed to Louisville’s economy, the discriminatory practices discussed earlier were taking place or would intensify in the following decades. Redlining, discriminatory policies, racial violence, and urban renewal were all limiting where many Black Louisvillians could live, while displacing residents farther west and contributing to an increasing concentration of Black residents in the West End.

One important detail about Rubbertown is that the surrounding neighborhoods were not always predominantly Black. According to an investigation by Louisville Public Media, the area surrounding Rubbertown was originally largely rural, while nearby neighborhoods such as Shawnee and Chickasaw were predominantly white. In fact, industrial pollution was already raising concerns as early as 1930, when Louisville's comprehensive city plan acknowledged that winds were carrying unpleasant odors from nearby oil refineries into Shawnee. The city's planner, Harland Bartholomew, recommended regulating or relocating the industries, but these changes were never implemented. Instead, over the following decades, white flight, discriminatory housing practices, and urban renewal contributed to major demographic changes throughout West Louisville. While many white families were increasingly able to move into suburban neighborhoods, Black Louisvillians continued facing barriers that restricted where they could purchase homes. This meant that many Black residents were living in communities already affected by decades of industrial pollution. How disproportionately were these communities affected? According to a 2019 investigation by Louisville Public Media, people living near toxic air polluters in Louisville were nearly three times as likely to be people of color and nearly twice as likely to live in poverty. These findings raise an important question: if the city was aware of industrial pollution decades earlier, why were the communities surrounding these industries left to face its consequences?

One of the most troubling pieces of evidence I found during my research concerns the environmental disparities in West Louisville. Between 2000 and 2001, researchers conducted the West Louisville Air Toxics Study, monitoring air pollution at 12 different locations throughout West Louisville. What did they find? According to Louisville Metro's Air Pollution Control District, the study's risk assessments found unacceptably high levels of toxic air pollutants in Louisville. Further monitoring between 2001 and 2005 led to a 2006 risk assessment that identified 11 chemicals exceeding a modeled lifetime cancer-risk benchmark of one in a million under at least one exposure scenario. These historical findings were not simply about unpleasant odors or minor environmental inconveniences. Researchers were identifying pollutants in the air that residents were breathing, with potential consequences for their long-term health. This raises another question: what did these historical exposures mean for the people who lived there, and what environmental risks remain for residents today? A recent study reported by the Courier Journal offers a troubling perspective. The Courier Journal found that asthma hospitalization rates in the West End were five times those in the East End. Furthermore, an analysis presented to an EPA environmental justice advisory meeting found that Black residents represented 39% of people in low-income, low-food-access areas within three miles of facilities covered by federal chemical accident-prevention rules, compared with 18% of Louisville's overall population. The concerns extend beyond air pollution and health outcomes. Louisville Metro's Urban Forestry Department states that formerly redlined neighborhoods with tree canopy coverage of 22% or less experienced temperatures up to 10°F higher than well-vegetated areas. This points to a relationship between historical redlining and present-day environmental conditions.

These findings are particularly troubling when considered alongside Louisville’s history. Environmental concerns are not unique to West Louisville or even to this city. I am not claiming these problems exist exclusively here. I am asking you to connect the dots and examine the evidence. For decades, Black Louisvillians faced discriminatory housing practices, the destruction of their neighborhoods, and restrictions on where they could live, through policy, and even violence from civilians. Now, generations later, many of these same communities face disproportionate environmental burdens and serious health disparities. While these studies do not prove that every health disparity was directly caused by historical policies, they reveal a troubling relationship between decisions made decades ago and the conditions residents experience today. So, when we look at the environmental inequalities present in West Louisville, we have to ask ourselves: are these simply unfortunate coincidences, or are they part of the consequences that occur when areas are segregated and given unequal investment?

Throughout this article, I have laid out facts about Louisville’s past—not to diminish or trash the city I love dearly, but to acknowledge a history that doesn’t get talked about enough. It is the history that my younger self unknowingly questioned so often. Acknowledging history doesn’t mean ignoring progress. I could write a whole other article on the progress Louisville has made as a city, and I suggest readers look into it on their own. We have invested more in the West End, worked to reduce air emissions since the 2000s, and established the West End Opportunity Partnership to invest in and develop more resources in the area. I could go on, but the point is that we haven’t done nothing. We’re getting somewhere, but that doesn’t mean the past gets erased. Nor does it mean that throwing money at these problems can undo decades of discrimination or erase the fact that Louisville remains a deeply segregated city. It’s not your fault or mine, but knowledge of how we got to where we are can better prepare us to get to where we want to be.

After all the evidence presented, you may ask why it matters. You may feel that it has nothing to do with you because it happened in the past. So I challenge everyone with this question: “If the people responsible for an injustice are long gone, but the consequences remain, can we really say that injustice is in the past?”

Think about the family who worked tirelessly to achieve the American dream we speak so highly of, only to be told that the neighborhood they dreamed of living in wasn’t meant for people like them. Or the Wade family, who pursued that dream despite discrimination, only to have their home shot at and bombed, while the people responsible for the bombing were never convicted. Think about the businesses on Walnut Street—not simply as storefronts offering goods and services, but as places built by owners, supported by workers who relied on their jobs, and sustained by customer relationships that took decades to develop. All of it was turned into rubble under the promise of progress. Think about the families whose communities were divided by highways and the children growing up with the burden of environmental pollution they never chose but still had to live with.

These were not mere statistics from a far-off land or tales from another time. This happened here, in our city, and its effects are still visible whether we want to acknowledge them or not. Most of all, these were people—people whose communities had already faced generations of discrimination.

When I speak about the consequences of history, I want everyone to understand something. A family denied the opportunity to buy a home wasn’t simply denied four walls and a roof; they were denied a chance to build equity, financial security, and potentially wealth to pass down to their children. Business owners and employees didn’t simply lose a storefront or a workplace. They lost relationships, years of work and growth, and the opportunity to leave something behind for the next generation. And when a community is divided, the streets are not the only things separated. The people and the connections between them are divided, too.

Now imagine this multiplied across families, neighborhoods, and generations. Then imagine someone being born into those conditions decades later, without ever having had a say in the decisions whose consequences they inherited.

This is why history matters.

This is not to make anyone feel guilty for something they did not do, nor is it to make you hate Louisville or feel ashamed to live here. I actually hope it makes you love the city more.

Love without knowledge is like admiring the beauty of a tree while refusing to acknowledge the roots from which it grew. It is dangerous. Blind love makes it nearly impossible to address something’s flaws because you refuse to understand its past or what made it the way it is.

We simply cannot claim to understand the inequalities within our city if we do not acknowledge how they got here in the first place. We cannot judge neighborhoods that experienced generations of disinvestment and pretend they arrived there by chance. We cannot celebrate the progress we have made without acknowledging the people who had to pay the price for the progress that came before it. And we cannot judge communities experiencing poor health without considering the pollution in the places they call home, or assume that their suffering is entirely their own fault. It would be inconsiderate and unjust to make any of these judgments without knowing the history behind them, and that's why I am writing this today.

The people who drew red lines and passed racist policies are largely gone. The businesses along Walnut Street are now part of history, and decades have passed since the Wade family’s home was bombed. You can’t change the past, but you can acknowledge the consequences of decisions that are still present today.

Most importantly, you have a choice about what happens next.

The question before us is not whether these things happened. They did, and the evidence is in the photographs, writings, laws, rhetoric, maps, neighborhoods, and testimonies of the people who lived through them.

The question now is what we can learn from all of this—and whether we are willing to learn from a history we did not personally experience.

What I hope you take away from this article is this:

“We may not be responsible for the sins of Louisville’s past, but we will be responsible if we allow their consequences to become the inheritance of its future.”
